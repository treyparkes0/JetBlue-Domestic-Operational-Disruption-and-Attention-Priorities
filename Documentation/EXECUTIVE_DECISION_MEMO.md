# Decision memo: where domestic operational attention should go first

**To:** Director, airline operations  
**From:** Trey Parkes  
**Date:** 8 November 2026  
**Source:** U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics, Reporting Carrier On-Time Performance, JetBlue (`B6`) only. Public data. Not an internal JetBlue extract.  
**Window:** 1 January 2018–31 July 2026. 2,110,783 domestic flights.

## The problem

JetBlue’s domestic disruption is not spread evenly. A late arrival, a cancellation, and a diversion are different events, and the file is large enough to show where each one concentrates. Limited attention should follow the minutes on busy places, with the rate read beside the minutes. A high rate on a thin place is a watch item.

In this window, 26.9% of operated flights arrived 15 minutes or more late, 2.40% of scheduled flights were cancelled, and 0.345% were diverted and not cancelled. Operated flights accumulated 44.2 million arrival-delay minutes. On the flights that were already late, 76.4% of the recorded cause minutes sit in the carrier and late-aircraft buckets. That is an association in the reporting categories. It is not proof that those minutes were avoidable, and it is not a cost.

## Where the minutes are

Five origins account for half of arrival-delay minutes:

| Origin | Flights | Late-arrival rate | Minutes |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| JFK | 317,477 | 27.8% | 6.52 million |
| Boston | 350,117 | 25.4% | 6.31 million |
| Fort Lauderdale | 170,468 | 30.0% | 3.78 million |
| Orlando | 144,635 | 30.2% | 3.51 million |
| San Juan | 82,442 | 28.8% | 1.93 million |

Boston flies more than JFK and arrives late less often. JFK still accounts for more minutes. Fort Lauderdale and Orlando are the origins where a high rate and a large operation sit together. San Juan is inside domestic scope and is fifth by minutes.

The same pattern shows up on routes. Florida–New York carries both the minutes and late-arrival rates near or above 31%. MCO → JFK is 612,862 minutes at 31.8%. The Boston–Washington shuttle carries a comparable minute load at 23.6% and 22.6%, because it flies more often. MCO → EWR is the sharp route-level case: 14,407 flights, 35.9% late, 434,041 minutes. FLL → EWR is 35.8% late on 10,993 flights.

DCA, LAX, and the JFK–LAX routes show the other side. They are busy, and their late-arrival rates are in the low 20s. They still belong in the first group because the minutes are large. They are not the reliability problem.

## Where reliability is elevated

Among origins with at least 1,000 flights, the top quartile starts at 29.59% late and 3.06% cancelled. Those are separate flags. A high cancellation rate is not ranked above a high minute total. Newark’s late-arrival rate is 29.6%, so it clears that cutoff.

Fort Lauderdale, Orlando, and Newark clear the late-arrival quartile and are also in the top 10 by minutes. Miami clears it without being in that top 10: 13,280 departures, 34.3% late, about 30 minutes of floored delay per operated flight. Denver is the same kind of item at 11,117 flights and 33.0% late.

LaGuardia is the cancellation case inside the minute top 10: 3.32% cancelled. Several smaller Northeast origins cancel more often than that and do not have a high late-arrival rate. Nantucket is the extreme, at 4.85% cancelled. Treat that as a cancellation question, not as evidence that those stations are the delay problem.

In 2022 through 2025 the same four origins stay at the top by minutes: JFK, Boston, Orlando, and Fort Lauderdale. Orlando moves ahead of Fort Lauderdale inside that window. LaGuardia replaces San Juan as fifth, with 1,050,166 minutes against San Juan’s 1,008,950. Over the full window San Juan is fifth and LaGuardia is seventh. That swap does not change the first review.

Six origins between 250 and 999 flights have late-arrival rates in the same range as the quartile: Asheville, Wilmington (NC), Hayden, Norfolk, Vero Beach, and Hyannis. They are monitoring items. Airports below 250 flights stay off the first list even when the rate looks extreme.

## What travels with the disruption

**Day of week.** Sunday is the worst day in this file: 29.5% late, 2.88% cancelled, and 7.37 million minutes. With 2020 removed, Sunday still leads, at 30.8% late, 2.79% cancelled, and 7.18 million minutes. Friday is the busiest day, at 314,540 flights and 28.6% late. Wednesday is the most reliable, at 23.5% late, and it is not the lightest day. Saturday is the lightest. A schedule review that assumes the busiest day is the problem, or that the quietest day is the recovery day, does not match these counts. The Sunday gap is seasonal. With 2020 removed, Sunday’s late-arrival rate sits above the other days by 7.8 points in January, 5.9 in February, 6.7 in March, 5.6 in November, and 4.5 in December. In July the gap is 0.9 points. In August it is 0.1 point. In September Sunday is 1.0 point lower than the other days. July is a high late-arrival month on every day. A Sunday review belongs with the winter operation. It does not explain July.

**Season.** July is the heaviest month on both the late-arrival rate (33.6%) and the minutes. With 2020 removed, July still leads, at 34.9% late and 5.67 million minutes. August and December both round to 32.0% late. August has 4,284,267 minutes and December has 3,942,031. October is the lightest late-arrival month (21.1%). March’s cancellation rate is high because 2020 is in the pool. With 2020 removed, January is the cancellation month, at 4.22%. July 2023 and July 2026 were severe individual months. April 2020 was a cancellation month, at 35.8%, on a shrunken schedule.

**Causes.** On late arrivals, late aircraft is 39.2% of cause minutes and carrier is 37.2%. NAS is 20.7%. The weather bucket is 2.7%, and that bucket is extreme weather only, not every weather minute inside NAS. Security delay minutes are 0.2%.

**Cancellations.** Weather is the largest cancellation code (36.1%), then carrier (28.6%) and NAS (22.0%). The security code is 13.3% of cancellations only because 6,755 of those 6,756 flights were cancelled in 2020. Of those, 6,749 are March and April, and six are in July, across the large origins. One security-coded cancellation appears after 2020, in May 2023. It is not a current security pattern. January is the weather-cancellation month. July is the NAS-cancellation month. In the partial 2026 year, weather is about half of cancellations so far.

## What to investigate first

1. **Review JFK and Boston first.** Together they hold 12.8 million arrival-delay minutes, 29% of the network’s 44.2 million. JFK has 317,477 flights and a 27.8% late-arrival rate. Boston has 350,117 flights and a 25.4% late-arrival rate. Boston flies more and is late less often. JFK still has more minutes. Ask whether turns and late aircraft explain that gap. The file does not show that cause. On late flights, the recorded mix is 39.2% late aircraft and 37.2% carrier. That mix does not prove those minutes were avoidable.
2. **Review MCO → JFK, MCO → EWR, and FLL → EWR in July, then August.** MCO → JFK is 22,110 flights, 31.8% late, and 612,862 minutes. MCO → EWR is 14,407 flights, 35.9% late, and 434,041 minutes. FLL → EWR is 10,993 flights, 35.8% late, and 299,164 minutes. July is first for the network and for these three routes together. August is second. Leave December off this list. June has more of these routes’ minutes than December does. For FLL → EWR, also look at April. April has more of that route’s minutes than July. July has the higher late-arrival rate.
3. **Review Sunday, then Friday.** Sunday has 312,745 flights, a 29.5% late-arrival rate, and 7.37 million minutes. Friday is the busiest day, with 314,540 flights, a 28.6% late-arrival rate, and 6.95 million minutes. The Sunday gap is large in winter and almost gone in July and August, so this review does not explain July.
4. **Review Miami for late arrivals.** 34.3% of Miami flights arrive late, on 13,280 flights. Miami is 22nd by total minutes, so this is a reliability review, not a minute review.
5. **Review LaGuardia for cancellations.** 2,168 of 65,340 flights were cancelled, 3.32%. LaGuardia is 7th by total minutes and the only top-10 minute airport above the 3.06% cancellation line. That line is the top quarter of cancellation rates among origins with at least 1,000 flights. Fort Myers is the next large airport on that cancellation list.

Do not start with the 2020 security-coded cancellations, including the six in July, with airports under 250 flights, or with a network-wide claim that a single change will cut the 76.4% carrier-plus-late-aircraft share. That share is a reporting association on flights that were already late. It is not a claim that those minutes were avoidable.

## What the workbook adds

`Jetblue_sensitivity_analysis.xlsx` compares ways to draw the attention list. It uses the origin ranking already in this memo. The plan, the first five origins by minutes, covers 22,045,754 minutes (49.9% of the network), 1,065,139 flights (50.5%), and 24,551 cancellations (48.4%). The five highest late-arrival rates cover 75,342 minutes (0.17%) on 2,340 flights, at a 35.8% late-arrival rate. That list has no flight minimum. Traverse City (74 flights) and Boise (39) are under 250 flights. Asheville (303) and Wilmington, NC (273) are in the 250–999 monitoring band. Newburgh (1,651) is the only origin on that list with at least 1,000 flights. Newark is the next airport by minutes. LaGuardia is the cancellation airport that list leaves off. Extending the minutes list to ten origins reaches 65.4% of minutes. The “minutes plus cancellation” option keeps those first five and adds one airport, so the covered list has six origins. The workbook cutoff for that option stays at five so the added airport is not counted twice.

Use the workbook to see what a different list would cover. It is not a saving, and it is not evidence that a program will deliver a cut.

## What this cannot establish

The file cannot support a dollar saving, a safety claim, an aircraft-type claim, or a statement that one management action will cause delay to fall. It cannot split 2026 marketed codeshares from operated flights. It cannot see inside the carrier bucket (crew, maintenance, baggage, and the other items reported there). It cannot treat 2020 as a normal year, and it cannot turn January–July 2026 into a full year. Origin and destination are separate, so an airport total that adds both roles double-counts flights.

The supporting counts, the quartile rules, and the test results are in `FINDINGS.md` and `METHODOLOGY.md`.
